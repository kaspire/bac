About the math — read me once

Baseline. On the first two cards from a full 8-deck shoe, a pair on one side ≈ 31/415 ≈ 7.47%. "Either side" ≈ 14.3%. These are what every analysis compares against.

House edge is the wall. Player/Banker pair pays 11:1 → edge ≈ 10.4%. "Either pair" 5:1 → edge ≈ 14%. No bet-sizing scheme changes the expected value of a negative-edge bet; a capped progression (1 unit, +1 per loss, stop at 10) only reshapes the distribution into many small wins and rarer larger losses. It will feel like a steady +1/shoe right up until it doesn't. That feeling is the system working as designed, not an edge.

"After a 7" and memory. The next two cards depend only on what remains in the shoe. The previous total has no causal link to the next pair beyond removing those specific cards — a tiny card-removal effect that is usually smaller than your sampling noise and does not reliably point any direction. If 7 is a common winning total, "pairs after 7" will look big purely because 7 is common. Always read the rate column, never the count.

Multiple comparisons. This tool tests ~10 totals × several streak lengths × several bands. If everything is pure noise, you should still expect roughly 1 in 20 cells to cross |z|>2 by chance. So treat a lone "notable" cell skeptically; only a stable effect that survives more data and shows up consistently is worth anything.

What this tool is good for: honestly measuring whether any of your patterns beat chance. What it can't do: make a 10–14% house edge disappear. Use it as a curiosity log and a noise-detector.
